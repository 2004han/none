Han-yu Zhang ,W e iX u , Member, IEEE , Qing-yu Peng, Dong-fang Wang , Cheng Jiang ,

Abstract— In robotic milling, monitoring surface rough-
ness in real-time is essential for maintaining the quality
of aircraft skin edges. However, existing techniques often
struggle with accuracy and robustness due to the com-
plex conditions during milling. This article introduces a
novel approach to real-time predict surface roughness in
robotic milling of aircraft skin edges. The method begins
by dynamically adjusting vibration signals using the fre-
quency response function to maintain prediction accuracy.
Subsequently, the phase space reconstruction is employed
to map the vibration signals from time domain into phase
space diffeomorphic to the robotic system, thereby un-
covering the system’s dynamic characteristics. Then, the
Gramian angular summation ﬁeld algorithm is used for fea-
ture extraction from the phase space trajectories, followed
by classiﬁcation using a convolutional neural network. A
vibration-roughness dataset is constructed based on prac-
tical experiments in order to train the proposed model.
The predictability of the machining system is demonstrated
quantitatively. Furthermore, the model’s practicality is val-
idated through online experiments. The prediction results
based on the test set conﬁrm that this approach offers sig-
niﬁcant improvements in prediction accuracy and robust-
ness over existing methods.
Index Terms— Aircraft skin parts, online roughness mon-
itoring, phase space reconstruction, robotic milling.

quality of skin edges is essential for maintaining the aerody-
namics, stealth properties, and structural integrity of the aircraft.
An excessively rough surface can compromise the tight contact
between skin components, impair surface sealing effectiveness,
and degrade the aircraft’s overall performance. Therefore, pre-
dicting surface roughness, especially in real-time during the
robotic milling process, is crucial for ensuring high-quality and
high efﬁciency aircraft manufacturing.
Surface roughness is typically measured ofﬂine by manually
selecting speciﬁc points on the machined surface for measure-
ment [6]. This approach, however, is not only time-consuming
but also prone to errors and inconsistencies, reducing its overall
efﬁciency. While statistical[7] and mathematical [8], [9] models
are often employed to predict roughness ofﬂine, these methods
generally fail to capture the dynamic changes that occur during
the machining process [10]. Consequently, their efﬁciency, ac-
curacy, and reliability are limited. In contrast, online roughness
monitoring enables real-time detection of machining irregulari-
ties during robotic milling, leading to enhanced production yield
and overall process efﬁciency.
A. Related Works
Online roughness monitoring is generally achieved indirectly
through vibration measurement which can be categorized into
noncontact and contact methods. Noncontact methods primarily
include acoustic emission [11], [12], laser Doppler vibrometry
[13], [14], and three-dimensional (3-D) point tracking with
structured light systems [15], [16]. Acoustic emission suffers
from low signal-to-noise ratios due to interference from indus-
trial environmental noise. Laser Doppler vibrometry exhibits
limited measurement accuracy for low-frequency noise [16]
and can only capture vibrations along the normal direction.
While 3-D point tracking and structured light systems offer a
broader measurement range, they demonstrate lower precision
and reduced signal-to-noise ratios [16]. Contact methods, such
as accelerometer and dynamometer, cannot directly measure the
machining area’s vibration but indirectly measures it at other
positions in the machining system, such as the ﬁxture [17],
spindle [18], and workpiece [19]. During the feeding of milling
tool, sensors installed at ﬁxed points cannot discern whether
changes in vibrations are caused by alterations in the machining
position or by variations in the surface roughness. Liu et al. [20]
correlated the cutting position with the monitoring signals of
cutting force and acceleration. Y ao et al.[21], [22] established a
position-dependent amplitude preprocessing function to adjust
the amplitude of vibration signals. However, due to the nonlinear
transmission characteristics of vibrations, the vibration propa-
gation not only affects the amplitude but also induces phase
shifts in vibration signals that remains uncompensated by the
aforementioned methodologies.
Signal-based prediction models extract features from the ac-
quired vibration signals through time-domain [26], [27], and
time-frequency domain [23], [24], [25] analyses. It mainly
includes short-time Fourier transform [23], wavelet transform
[24], Hilbert Huang transform [25], Gramian angular ﬁeld [26],
and phase space reconstruction [27]. Recent years, AI-based
methods have gained attention for their ﬂexibility, practical-
ity, and stronger feature extraction ability [28]. For instance,
Zhao et al. [29] developed an long short-term memory (LSTM)
network for estimating material removal rates from vibration
acceleration signals. Cao et al. [30] created a sparse Bayesian
regression model to monitor machining errors by analyzing
real-time milling force, cutting parameters, and positions. Lu
et al. [31] used a convolutional neural network (CNN) classiﬁer
to link vibration signals with machined surface images, while Li
et al. [32] proposed a deep learning model incorporating machin-
ing parameters and milling force signals to enhance prediction
accuracy. He et al. [33] applied an symmetrized dot pattern
(SDP)-CNN framework to accurately predict grinding quality
from vibration signals. Existing methods rely on single-channel
signal or simple combinations of multichannel signals, employ-
ing feature extractors to achieve prediction. These approaches
exhibit high sensitivity to noise and lack a comprehensive anal-
ysis of the nonlinear characteristics embedded in signals.
B. Contribution
This article presents a comprehensive framework for rough-
ness prediction in robot milling of aircraft skin edges, com-
prising an FRF-based vibration signal correction method and a
phase space reconstruction Gramian angular summation ﬁeld
CNN (PSR-GASF-CNN) roughness monitoring model. The
vibration signal correction method compensates for vibration
signals acquired by accelerometer mounted at ﬁxed point using
frequency response functions (FRFs) of discretized milling seg-
ments. Time-domain vibration signals are mapped as trajectories
in the phase space through PSR method, with GASF employed
to extract features from these trajectories. A CNN classiﬁer
then performs recognition and classiﬁcation, enabling precise
prediction of surface roughness. The main contributions of this
method are summarized as follows.
1) Enhanced Accuracy and Robustness: Introduce vibration
signal correction method into roughness prediction to
prevent accuracy degradation caused by tool path dis-
placement. Moreover, the GASF method encodes global

II. FRF-B ASED VIBRA TIONSIGNAL CORRECTION METHOD
During robotic milling of skin parts, ﬁxed position sensors
cannot adapt to changes in machining points. To overcome this
challenge, we propose a vibration signal correction method. By
discretizing the skin edge and separately measuring the FRFs
between each discretized point and the accelerometer, position-
dependent correction can be applied to the vibration signals.
This method facilitates precise monitoring of roughness.
A. Vibration T ransmission Model
In the robotic milling of large aircraft skin parts, clamping
methods such as magnets and vacuum suction cups are often
used to accommodate the scale and complex curvatures of
these components. However, these methods do not meet the
boundary conditions required for accurate theoretical analysis
in dynamics [34]. To address the difﬁculties in creating precise
vibration transmission models for roughness monitoring, this
section introduces a vibration transmission model based on the
vibration FRF. This approach provides an alternative to theoret-
ical methods, enabling more reliable acquisition of vibrations
during robotic milling of aircraft skin parts.
Fig. 2 presents a vibration transmission model of skin parts
edge milling. For aircraft skin parts, the stiffness in the x−
andy− directions are much greater than that in the z-direction.
However, the clamping method based on magnets and vacuum
suction cups results in lower clamping stiffness. During actual
machining processes, signiﬁcant vibrations exist in all three
directions, x, y, and z. Hence, the transmission model needs
to consider vibrations in all three directions.
During the robotic milling process of aircraft skin parts’ edge,
the position of the milling tool varies over time. However, within
a sampling period, the movement distance of milling tool is
much smaller than the size of the workpiece. As a result, during

Due to the diffeomorphism, phase space can more directly
present the nonlinear dynamic characteristics of robotic machin-
ing systems compared to time and frequency domains, which is
beneﬁcial for extracting features with high identiﬁcation [35].
Therefore, establishing a roughness monitoring model based
on phase space trajectory is crucial to enhance the predictive
performance of the model.

C. Online Monitoring Experiment
To validate the applicability of the proposed roughness mon-
itoring model, a practical online monitoring experiment is con-
ducted using the robotic machining system described in Sec-
tion IV -A. The use of a variable feed rate machining strategy
allows for the generation of different surface roughness levels,
which helps analyze the sensitivity of the model to roughness
changes. The RDC and ADC are ﬁxed at 2.0 mm. In this strategy,
the feed rate for the A-B-C segment is set to 3 mm/s, while for
the C-D-E segment, it is set to 1 mm/s, as shown in Fig. 14(a).
The edge regions (i.e., before point A and after point E) are
excluded from the analysis due to structural instability.
By applying the prediction model trained in the previous
section, we are able to perform online monitoring of surface
roughness of the skin parts. The predicted results are shown
in Fig. 14(b), with points A to E marked. In the predicted
results, the B-C segment is located between two magnets, where
the clamping stiffness is higher, resulting in lower roughness
compared to the A-B segment. At point C, the model accurately
responds to the roughness changes caused by the abrupt feed
rate change. Point D is closer to the magnet, where the clamping
stiffness is optimal, and as a result, this point exhibits the best
machining quality.
At each feature point, ten discrete points are selected in the
vicinity, and their surface roughness is measured. The results
indicate that the model has a higher prediction accuracy in
the low-speed segments (nearly 100%) and a lower accuracy
in the variable-speed machining segments (only 60%). The
overall prediction accuracy is 90%, lower than that of the
test set. This discrepancy can be attributed to the randomness
and instability inherent in the actual machining process, which
affects the model’s predictive performance. Overall, the pro-
posed model provides real-time surface roughness monitoring,
promptly identiﬁes robotic milling anomalies of aircraft skin
edge.
V. C ONCLUSION
We propose a PSR-GASF-CNN-based roughness monitoring
model for online surface roughness assessment in aircraft skin
edge milling. This model employs position-dependent FRFs to
correct vibration signals, effectively suppressing the inﬂuence
of tool feed motion along the workpiece. The methodology
reconstructs vibration signals into phase space through delay
embedding. When integrated with CNN classiﬁers, the global
relationships of phase space trajectories are encoded via GASF,
achieving over 96.7% accuracy on test sets. The model demon-
strates strong robustness, maintaining above 90% accuracy in
high-noise environments. Additionally, the predictability of vi-
bration signals is validated through LLE and KE analyses. While
current model focuses on rectangular planar workpieces, and
future research will extend this framework to free-form surface
workpieces. Overall, this solution successfully addresses online
roughness monitoring requirements of aircraft skins and offers
substantially guidance for industrial implementation.
